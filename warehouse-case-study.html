The Challenge

A third party WMS with no room to improve

Third-Party Dependency

Operators used a vendor-managed Zebra WMS. BigBasket had zero control over UX, workflow logic, or the ability to iterate and improve.

High Cognitive Load

Excessive actions per screen increased cognitive load, requiring operators to make system-level decisions.

Hidden Inventory Data

Checking QOH, batch info or bin locations meant switching screens or walking to a desktop terminal mid-task, under time pressure.

Manual FMCG / FNV Flow

Found FMCG and FNV stock had no digital process. Workers improvised, causing inventory mismatches and write off delays.

The Before State

What operators dealt with every single shift

6 action buttons with zero visual hierarchy

HOLD, CLOSE CRATE, REFRESH, GENERATE EXTRA BARCODE, SWAP POSITION, POST — all on one screen. Operators decided what to press under time pressure.

Information density with no grouping logic

Cart, QOH, Location, Order No, SubType, Position, Pick Qty all at equal visual weight. The eye has no natural path through the screen.

Two competing scan fields shown simultaneously

'Scan Product' and 'Scan Position' both visible at once. Workers frequently scanned the wrong field without immediate feedback.

No FIFO batch logic surfaced to the operator

Batch expiry and manufacturing dates were hidden. Operators manually decided which stock to pick introducing human error into inventory integrity.

External vendor zero iteration control

Any UX issue required a vendor request. BigBasket could not fix, improve or adapt the tool to match their warehouse operations.

03 · Research

Field Observation

No brief document existed. The only way to understand the problem was to see it live.

What I observed on the floor

Workers operated the Zebra device one-handed while physically handling stock gloves on, moving fast.
The legacy app required them to read through dense text to find the bin location. Many had memorised common locations to avoid reading it at all.
Several operators pressed the wrong button more than once per session. The coloured buttons (red CLOSE CRATE, green REFRESH, orange GENERATE) created confusion rather than guidance.
The field visit made it immediately clear: the app needed to show one instruction at a time, let the scanner do the work, and eliminate every optional decision from the screen.
My lead reviewed my observations and helped me validate the direction fewer steps, guided flow, scanner-initiated actions.
Live Experience > Assumptions

Seeing operators use the Zebra device in real warehouse lighting, with gloves, under time pressure immediately invalidated the desk assumptions I started with.

Click Reduction as the Core Goal

Every extra tap is a warehouse costs seconds at scale. The field visit made click reduction the primary success metric not visual aesthetics.

The Visit Defined the Flow

The one-instruction-at-a-time principle, the persistent SKU header, and the scan entry point all came directly from watching operators on the floor not from a brief.

Scanner-First

Every flow starts with a scan. No manual typing. Scanner initiates all key actions.

Persistent SKU Context

SKU ID and description anchored at the top of every screen. Operators never lose track.

System Suggestions

Suggested qty and bin locations surface from the backend. Operators confirm, not calculate.

Session Timers

20-min countdown prevents ghost tasks. Supervisor throughput stays predictable.

Confirm Before Commit

All destructive or irreversible actions require modal confirmation. Errors in stacking are expensive.

Impact & Outcome

Deployment & Adoption for Warehouse Operations

191+ Screens Delivered — across 7 modules

Field Visit Overview

No project document existed. Going to the warehouse in person gave me the brief, the user, the constraint, and the primary design direction all in one visit.

Click Reduction for Operational Efficiency

In a warehouse, every extra tap is a cost. Operational UX is not about aesthetics — it's about eliminating decisions that the system should already have made.

The Power of Modular Applications

Splitting into focused apps eliminated mode confusion. Operators launched the right tool, did their task, and closed it. No navigation, no menus, no wrong flows.

Warehouse Operations Learning Project

Designing for warehouse ops taught me inventory lifecycle, FIFO logic, batch traceability, and DC operations knowledge that made every design decision better informed.
